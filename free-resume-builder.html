Resume Knowledge Guide for Beginners

Not sure what to write in your resume? Open only the topic you need. Each answer expands without forcing you to scroll through a long page.

CV stands for Curriculum Vitae. It is generally a more detailed record of a person's education, academic background, experience, achievements, research, publications, or other professional history.

The exact meaning and expected length can vary by country and profession. Do not assume that every employer wants a long CV.

A resume is a concise summary of your most relevant education, skills, experience, projects, and achievements for a particular job.

For most ordinary job applications, the goal is not to tell your entire life story. The goal is to show the employer why your background fits the role.

There is no universal rule. Follow the employer's instructions first. If a job posting asks for a resume, submit a resume. If it specifically asks for a CV, provide a CV.

For a normal private-sector job application where no format is specified, a concise, job-focused resume is usually the practical choice. Academic, research, medical, or other specialized applications may require a more detailed CV.

Simple rule: Job advertisement says “Resume” → choose Resume. It says “CV” → choose CV. It gives no instruction → choose the concise format that best presents your relevant qualifications.

Bio-Data usually focuses more on personal and biographical information. It may be requested for particular applications, forms, or contexts where details such as date of birth, gender, marital status, or other personal information are relevant.

Do not add sensitive or unnecessary personal details to a job resume just because the builder has optional fields. Include information when it is relevant or requested.

A Career Objective is a short statement describing the kind of role you are seeking and the value you want to bring to the employer. It is especially useful for many freshers who have limited professional experience.

A good objective should be specific enough to show direction rather than saying only “I want a challenging job.” Mention the role, relevant strengths, and what you hope to contribute.

Example approach: “Seeking an entry-level web development role where I can apply my JavaScript and problem-solving skills while contributing to practical projects.”

A Professional Summary is a short overview of your relevant experience, skills, achievements, and professional direction. It is often more useful than a generic objective for experienced candidates.

Keep it focused on evidence and relevance to the job instead of using vague adjectives.

Skills are abilities you can use to perform tasks or solve problems. A fresher does not need years of employment to have useful skills.

Look at your college projects, internships, assignments, volunteer work, hobbies with practical output, software you have actually used, and tasks you can perform independently.

  • Used Excel to organize and analyze data → Excel / Data Handling
  • Built a website → HTML, CSS, JavaScript / Web Development
  • Presented a project to a class → Presentation / Communication
  • Worked with classmates to complete a project → Teamwork
Important: Do not copy a skill simply because it appears in a job description. Write skills you genuinely have and can explain.

Technical Skills are specific tools, technologies, methods, or job-related abilities—for example Excel, Photoshop, SQL, JavaScript, accounting software, machine operation, or video editing.

Soft Skills describe how you work with people and tasks—for example communication, teamwork, adaptability, problem solving, organization, or time management.

Use both when they are genuinely relevant to the job, but support important claims with examples in your experience, projects, or interview.

Do not invent employment experience. Instead, strengthen the sections that show what you can do: education, projects, internships, certifications, technical skills, relevant coursework, achievements, volunteering, and genuine responsibilities.

If you completed a meaningful college or personal project, explain what you built, what tools you used, and what your contribution was.

Yes. Internships are relevant practical experience and can be clearly identified as internships. Projects can also demonstrate practical ability, especially for freshers.

Describe your actual contribution, tools used, and result. Do not present a college project as paid employment if it was not.

Your resume creates expectations for the interviewer. If you list a skill, project, job responsibility, certification, or achievement, be prepared to explain it accurately.

Golden rule: Never add a skill just to impress an ATS or recruiter if you cannot explain how you learned it or where you used it.

Update your resume whenever something meaningful changes: a new job, internship, project, certification, skill, achievement, qualification, or responsibility.

It is also wise to review it before an important job search so outdated information, old contact details, or irrelevant skills can be removed.

Keep your core facts consistent, but customize the resume for relevant jobs. Prioritize the skills, projects, experience, and keywords that genuinely match the job description.

Customization does not mean inventing qualifications. It means presenting your real qualifications in the order most relevant to that role.

ATS keywords are job-related terms that appear in a job description and may help a screening system or recruiter identify relevant qualifications.

Use keywords naturally when they accurately describe your experience or skills. Do not hide keywords, repeat them unnaturally, or claim skills you do not have.

There is no universal page limit. A fresher can often communicate the important information in one focused page, while an experienced candidate may need more space for relevant experience.

The better rule is: include relevant information, remove repetition, and make important evidence easy to find.

It depends on the employer, country, profession, and application instructions. For many ATS-based corporate applications, a text-focused resume without a photo can be preferable. Some hospitality, aviation, entertainment, or bio-data contexts may specifically request one.

If a photo is not requested, check the employer's expectations before adding personal information.

  1. Check your name, phone number, email, and location.
  2. Make sure dates and job titles are accurate.
  3. Check spelling, grammar, and formatting.
  4. Remove skills or claims you cannot explain.
  5. Make sure the resume matches the job you are applying for.
  6. Open the final PDF/Word file and inspect it before sending.
  7. Make sure your resume and interview answers will tell the same truthful story.

Yes. You can use education, projects, internships, certifications, skills, achievements, and other genuine experience to present your qualifications.
